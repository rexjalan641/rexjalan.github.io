Germany decided not to build an AI super-regulator. Here's why that matters.

When the EU AI Act passed, most people assumed every member state would eventually build the same thing, a powerful centralized AI agency with one building, one logo and one phone number.

On 29 July 2026 Germany switched on its national enforcement machinery and took a different path. The Bundesnetzagentur (BNetzA), Germany's network regulator, became the central authority, while banks stayed with their bank supervisor, broadcasters with their media authorities, and a new coordination unit now holds the system together.

This piece explains what Germany built, why it distributed oversight across specialists instead of concentrating it, and what the choice means for companies, complainants and every other EU country still designing its own enforcement law.

2. What actually happened

Three things became law on 29 July 2026. The Act on the Market Surveillance and Support of Innovation of Artificial Intelligence (KI-MIG) entered into force as Germany's domestic implementation of the EU AI Act. As the name suggests, it carries twin mandates of market surveillance and innovation promotion with equal legal weight.

BNetzA took on three roles at once under this law. It became the AI Act's market surveillance authority for sensitive areas such as AI in employment, critical infrastructure, education and radio equipment. It also became the single point of contact for the whole ecosystem and the central complaints office. Its president Klaus Müller framed the posture deliberately in these words:

"The AI Act is a milestone for the responsible use of artificial intelligence in Europe … We see ourselves as an enabler and point of contact for the whole AI ecosystem." (official English edition, BNetzA press release, 29 July 2026)

However, the sectors kept their own watchdogs. While BNetzA serves as the default authority, sectoral supervisors keep their domains, so per § 2 of the statute financial-sector AI stays with the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin), media services remain with the state media authorities, and public bodies answer to state-level authorities. A new Coordination and Competence Centre for the AI Regulation (KoKIVO) also sits inside BNetzA to make the system cohere through shared expertise, uniform answers to cross-cutting legal questions, training and stakeholder involvement.

3. Why distribute the oversight?

Germany had 2 models on the table. One option was concentrating all AI oversight in a single powerful agency, and the other was distributing it across existing specialists with central coordination. Germany chose the second path, and the reasoning matters because every EU member state faces the same choice.

Concentration has real appeal in that one address takes the blame, standards stay consistent and expertise piles up in one place. Distribution matches how AI actually shows up in the economy — embedded in banking, medical devices, broadcasting and employment. When cars gained computers, vehicle safety regulators learned the software side themselves because they already understood the vehicles. Germany applies the same logic in betting on regulators who already know each sector, supported by a shared pool of AI expertise that KoKIVO provides.

Every design choice gives something up, and distributed oversight spreads accountability thin across many desks. When harm crosses sectors, say an AI hiring tool used by a bank, deciding which supervisor owns the case requires coordination where a single command structure would decide by default. The first cross-sector enforcement cases will show whether Germany's design holds.

4. What changes in practice

If you sell AI systems in Germany: your complaints exposure now has one front door. Anyone can file an AI Act complaint with BNetzA regardless of which authority ends up competent, BNetzA triages and forwards it (§ 8(2)) and must tell complainants where the case went. Which authority supervises you depends on your sector, and the sector map in § 2 of the statute tells you where you stand.

If you want to file a complaint: you now have a single federal address, and the authority has a legal duty to route the case somewhere accountable.

For other member states: Germany is the largest EU market to legislate on this, and its choice of distributed oversight with one visible center offers a template other capitals can copy. Brussels should therefore expect fifteen national answers to the same design question, coordinated rather than consolidated.

5. What to watch

Resourcing. No headcount or budget has been published for BNetzA's AI unit, the decision chamber or KoKIVO, and capacity remains an open question without those numbers.

The first annual report. The three-member KI-Marktüberwachungskammer (AI Market Surveillance Chamber) decides enforcement cases insulated by statute from external influence (§ 4(3)). Its report to the Bundestag covering 2026 is due 31 March 2027 and will be the earliest hard evidence of enforcement.

Federal-state delegation. German states may place AI supervision with BNetzA through a mechanism called Organleihe, meaning borrowing of the federal agency. Each state currently runs its own authorities, and states that lack AI expertise can instead use BNetzA's staff by formal agreement. The list of states taking the option over time shows how far consolidation goes in practice.

BaFin's next move. The press release names BaFin only in passing, and the way its financial mandate operates through supervisory notices and guidance will show whether sectoral supervisors pick up the AI file.
